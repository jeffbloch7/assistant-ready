Staying safe with AI

These are the steps I set up for myself and my clients before connecting an AI assistant to anything. Free to use, and free to share.

Last reviewed October 2026 · General guidance, not professional security advice.

1. Lock down your accounts first

Your email account is the master key to everything else. Start there.

  • Turn on two-step verification

    Use an authenticator app or passkeys rather than text-message codes.

    Google · Apple · Microsoft

  • Use passkeys where offered

    They're easier than passwords and much harder to phish.

    Google passkeys

  • Use a password manager

    The one built into your browser or phone counts.

    Google Password Manager

  • Run a security checkup

    See what's signed in to your account, and check whether your email address has shown up in a data breach.

    Google Security Checkup · Have I Been Pwned

  • Extra protection if you're a bigger target

    Business owners, anyone who handles money, and people in the public eye: consider physical security keys, which you plug in or tap to sign in. Google's Advanced Protection is its strongest setting.

    Apple security keys · Google Advanced Protection

2. Connecting an AI assistant safely

The part most security guides skip.

  • Connect only what you'll use

    Start with read-only access, and add more only when you need it.

  • Know how to see and remove access

    Every app you've connected to your account is listed in one place. Check it a few times a year and remove anything you don't recognize.

    Google connections · Microsoft app permissions

  • Don't paste these into a chat

    Passwords, verification codes, full card or bank numbers, and other people's private information.

  • Emails and web pages can carry hidden instructions

    A message can try to trick an assistant into doing something you didn't ask for. Keep approvals on for actions like sending email or buying things.

    Anthropic on prompt injection

  • Check your privacy settings

    See what's stored and how to control it.

    Anthropic Privacy Center

3. AI-era scams to watch for

  • Fake tech support

    Anthropic, Google, Apple and Microsoft won't call you about a problem with your computer. A real security warning never asks you to call a number.

    FTC: Tech support scams

  • A "family member" in trouble

    AI can copy a voice from a short clip. Hang up and call them back on a number you already know, and agree on a family code word.

    FTC: Family emergency scams · FTC: Voice cloning

  • Phishing with perfect spelling

    AI has removed the typos that used to give scams away. Go to the company's website or app yourself instead of clicking the link.

    FTC: Phishing

  • "Move your money to keep it safe"

    Always a scam, whether the caller says they're your bank, the government or the police.

    FTC: Imposter scams

  • Fake invoices and changed bank details

    For businesses: before paying a new invoice or new payment details, call the supplier on a number you already have.

    FTC: Pay your bills, not scammers

4. If something goes wrong

  • Secure the account

    Change the password, sign out of all other devices, and remove any connected apps you don't recognize (links in section 2).

  • Report it

    Report scams to the FTC and internet crime to the FBI. Forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org and phishing texts to 7726 (SPAM).

    ReportFraud.ftc.gov · FBI IC3
